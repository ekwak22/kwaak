THE GUIDING PHILOSOPHY

“Let’s Live Together on this Planet Earth”

A philosophy of care, service and shared belonging. The vision uses URI to mean both “Cosmic Order” and “We”: each person is unique, and our lives are connected.

A PHILOSOPHY IN PRACTICE

Live. Build. Serve. Connect.

The new vision outline brings four actions together: live together, build a sustainable world, serve others and connect people. Kwaak calls each person “Uriwebuna”—a unique being with a place and a purpose in the wider world.

Keep learning

Question prejudice and mistaken assumptions; learn again to become better.

Serve with people

His idea of “Servernance” places service among people at the heart of public life.

Make room for difference

Respect distinct beliefs and ways of life while seeking shared care for the planet.

1958 / Life & philosophy

The promise that came first

A young person’s answer to the landscape left by war.

HIS CONTRIBUTION

A lifelong commitment to peaceful coexistence

In 1958, while still in middle school, Kwaak spoke with his elder brother about how they might contribute to Korea’s future. His brother imagined a constitutional framework for reunification. Kwaak imagined a place where people from North and South could live together: a peace city near the DMZ.

Architecture appealed to him because it brought many interests into one field — geography, engineering, art, politics, landscape and education. Designing a city meant thinking about the conditions of everyday life, not only its buildings.

That early promise connects the different scales of his work: the individual, the nation and the wider world. The peace city remained an aspiration, but the question of how to live together continued to guide his planning and civic work.

Miracle on the Han River, English edition, p. 11.

Learning / Education

Learning to bring the world home

An exchange visit grew into a wider education — and a strategy for national development.

HIS CONTRIBUTION

MIT architecture; Harvard policy and education studies; teaching in Korea

In 1962, Kwaak traveled to the United States as a Korean representative in the Youth Red Cross Operation VISTA exchange. Seeing a different world strengthened his determination to study abroad and contribute to Korea’s development.

He completed undergraduate and graduate architecture studies at MIT, pursued public policy and education at Harvard, and later connected practice with teaching in Korea. His Korean biography also records a doctorate in education from Dongguk University.

A visit to the 1964 New York World’s Fair gave him a lasting idea: if young Koreans could not all travel abroad to see the future, an international exposition could bring that experience to Korea. Major events could become a reason to build lasting infrastructure as well as a meeting place for cultures.

Miracle on the Han River, English edition, pp. 2, 12, 15–18; Korean biography, education section.

Taekwondo / Taekwondo

Discipline shared through teaching

Taekwondo and civic service add a personal dimension to a life of planning.

HIS CONTRIBUTION

Ninth-degree Taekwondo black belt; educator and civic organizer

At the end of his studies in the United States, Kwaak founded the MIT–Harvard Taekwondo Club and taught students and professors. He recalls using the money he earned to travel to Europe and study the 1972 Munich Olympics in person, photographing venues and making notes.

His biography records a ninth-degree black belt and leadership in the Chung Do Kwan World Federation. The same thread of teaching and service appears in his founding work for the Beautiful School Movement and the clean public restroom civic movement.

These activities place his larger projects in a human context: learning through practice, sharing knowledge, and paying attention to the ordinary settings in which people live.

Miracle on the Han River, English edition, p. 18; Korean biography, civic activities.
